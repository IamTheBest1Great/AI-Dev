## 1. Action Verification Fundamentals

**Q1. [Conceptual] Explain Action Verification Fundamentals in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** Action verification is the process of checking that an action is permitted and well specified before execution, then gathering evidence of what actually happened afterward.


**Q2. [Interview] Why does Action Verification Fundamentals matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** Suppose the user asks, “Mark task T-104 as reviewed.” The task service may commit that field and then asynchronously update a dashboard. The database write can be complete while the dashboard remains stale. A useful answer identifies the completed effect and any material pending work instead of treating the entire system as a single Boolean.

## 2. Before Execution: Preconditions and Authority

**Q3. [Conceptual] Explain Before Execution: Preconditions and Authority in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** Define the action, target, material arguments, environment, and expected result. “Fix the project” is underspecified; “Set T-104's review flag to true” can become a narrow update.


**Q4. [Interview] Why does Before Execution: Preconditions and Authority matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** An HTTP API can use If-Match with a strong entity tag to condition an update on a particular representation version. Under RFC 9110, Section 13.1.1, a failed condition normally prevents the operation and yields a precondition failure. If-Match: checks existence, not a particular revision.

### Preconditions describe the state that makes the action valid

<a id="4-operation-identity-and-idempotency-keys"></a>
## 4. Operation Identity and Idempotency Keys

**Q15. [Conceptual] Explain Operation Identity and Idempotency Keys in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** A logical operation is one intended business action. An attempt is one delivery of that action to a service. Several attempts may belong to one operation because responses can be lost.


**Q16. [Interview] Why does Operation Identity and Idempotency Keys matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** The service also records a canonical payload fingerprint. If an existing key arrives with a different target, desired state, or expected version, it rejects the mismatch instead of silently changing the operation.


<a id="5-execution-boundaries-and-recorded-effects"></a>
## 5. Execution Boundaries and Recorded Effects

**Q17. [Conceptual] Explain Execution Boundaries and Recorded Effects in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** For changes in one transactional database, commit the mutation and completion receipt together. The record should not describe a successful effect until the effect and record are part of the same successful transaction.


**Q18. [Interview] Why does Execution Boundaries and Recorded Effects matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** If the assistant times out locally, it should preserve the operation identity and investigate status. Reporting “cancelled” merely because the user interface stopped waiting would assert more than the available evidence supports.

## 6. After Execution: Results and Postconditions

**Q19. [Conceptual] Explain After Execution: Results and Postconditions in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** Validate network, protocol, tool, and business outcomes separately; reassuring free text is insufficient.


**Q20. [Interview] Why does After Execution: Results and Postconditions matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** A receipt should come from the trusted service through an authenticated channel. Validate its tenant scope, operation ID, target, command fingerprint or equivalent binding, outcome, and resulting version. Do not accept an identifier supplied by untrusted task text as proof that an operation succeeded.

### Asynchronous and partial completion

**Q25. [Conceptual] Explain Asynchronous and partial completion in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** HTTP 202 Accepted means processing has been accepted but is not complete; RFC 9110, Section 15.3.3 makes that distinction explicit. Follow the documented status mechanism and report pending work honestly.


**Q26. [Interview] Why does Asynchronous and partial completion matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** For batches, inspect every item's outcome. Suppose ten task updates yield seven committed, two rejected, and one unknown. “The request succeeded” is an inadequate summary. Record the seven receipts, explain the two rejections, and reconcile the unknown item. Retrying the entire batch with new operation keys risks repeating the seven successful effects.

## 7. Ambiguous Timeouts and Reconciliation

**Q29. [Conceptual] Explain Ambiguous Timeouts and Reconciliation in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** An ambiguous outcome occurs when available evidence cannot determine whether an operation took effect. A timeout after dispatch is the classic example: the service may have rejected the request, may still be processing it, or may have committed it before losing the response.


**Q30. [Interview] Why does Ambiguous Timeouts and Reconciliation matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** A receipt index might lag behind the transaction store. A timed-out request might still be queued. A status endpoint may retain records for less time than the client retains operations. Therefore “not found” can mean more than “never happened.”.

<a id="8-safe-retries-and-duplicate-prevention"></a>
## 8. Safe Retries and Duplicate Prevention

**Q35. [Conceptual] Explain Safe Retries and Duplicate Prevention in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** A retry is safe only under known conditions. Relevant questions include whether the operation is inherently idempotent, whether the service honors an idempotency key, whether the original payload remains unchanged, whether the deduplication window is valid, and whether authorization still permits the attempted access.


**Q36. [Interview] Why does Safe Retries and Duplicate Prevention matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** Changing the payload while retaining the key should produce a conflict. A changed desired state is a new logical operation and needs its own authority and applicable review. Do not use a new key simply to escape a mismatch error while the original action remains ambiguous.


<a id="9-compensation-undo-and-escalation"></a>
## 9. Compensation, Undo, and Escalation

**Q37. [Conceptual] Explain Compensation, Undo, and Escalation in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** An undo restores a previous state when that restoration is supported and appropriate. A compensation is a new business action that offsets a completed action's consequences as far as possible.


**Q38. [Interview] Why does Compensation, Undo, and Escalation matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** A safe recovery plan checks the current state and the scope of recovery authority. It may condition restoration on the task still having the mistaken assignment. If the condition fails, present the specific conflict to an authorized reviewer rather than guessing whose change should win.

## 10. Runnable Transaction and Receipt Example

**Q39. [Conceptual] Explain Runnable Transaction and Receipt Example in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** Authenticated principals and project grants are trusted test fixtures. This example assumes the concrete action already passed the intent and applicable approval gates described in M08.2.1 and M08.5.1. It still checks current object access before mutation or receipt replay. There is no endpoint that trusts a client-supplied approval Boolean.


**Q40. [Interview] Why does Runnable Transaction and Receipt Example matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** Changed arguments with the same key fail. A new key with the stale version also fails. Access revocation prevents replay from exposing the receipt. The fixture therefore separates duplicate recognition, concurrency protection, and permission enforcement.

## 15. L3 Project and Acceptance Evidence

**Q47. [Conceptual] Explain L3 Project and Acceptance Evidence in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** Build a fictional task-service adapter that marks one task reviewed and optionally schedules a notification obligation. Include tenant A and tenant B, two projects within tenant A, versioned tasks, a receipt ledger, and deterministic access rules. Use an isolated test environment.
