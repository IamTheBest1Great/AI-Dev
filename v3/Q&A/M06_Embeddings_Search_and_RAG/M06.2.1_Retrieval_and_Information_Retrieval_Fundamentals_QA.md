## 1. Core insight

**Q21. [Conceptual] Explain Core insight in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** Retrieval is not "search for similar text." It's the design of a pipeline that finds eligible, relevant evidence — where eligibility (authorization, version, lifecycle state) is enforced independently of relevance (how well something matches the query), and no downstream stage can fix a failure that happened upstream.


**Q22. [Interview] Why does Core insight matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** Find what's eligible, rank what's relevant, never let a downstream stage paper over an upstream miss.


**Q23. [Scenario] A developer misunderstands Core insight while building a multi-tenant RAG assistant over versioned training manuals. How would you correct the design?**

**Answer:** Clarify the actual behavior: A reranker cannot recover a document the first-stage retriever never found. RRF cannot fix a wrong candidate. Evaluation is the stage that tells you which earlier stage actually broke. Demonstrate the difference with a concrete input and expected result before changing the system.

## 3. Stage cards

**Q25. [Conceptual] Explain Stage cards in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** Purpose: decide what counts as a match and how matches should be ordered. Key decisions: Boolean vs phrase vs fuzzy matching; TF-IDF/BM25 as the ranking baseline; field boosting weights; which conditions are filters (mandatory) vs boosts (preference); Top-k budget at each handoff point. Failure modes: fuzzy-matching two different identifiers into each other; treating a security condition as a boost instead of a filter; facet counts leaking the existence of unauthorized documents;.


**Q26. [Interview] Why does Stage cards matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** Purpose: turn what the user typed into what the system should actually search for, without changing their intent or their authorization. Key decisions: expansion vs rewriting vs multi-query vs self-querying; how many reformulations are worth the latency; what self-querying is allowed to propose vs what only the trusted backend may decide. Failure modes: query drift (expanding "port" into networking instead of harbor); a rewrite silently dropping negation or flipping a date condition;.

## 3. Tokenization and Linguistic Processing

**Q53. [Conceptual] Explain Tokenization and Linguistic Processing in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** Stop words are common terms that some systems remove or treat specially. Examples in English include articles and prepositions. Removing them can reduce index noise, but it can also damage phrases or meaning.


**Q54. [Interview] Why does Tokenization and Linguistic Processing matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** Words such as “not,” “without,” “before” and “after” may be critical in procedures. A generic stop-word list should not be applied blindly to every domain. Modern systems can retain common terms when they are useful; stop-word removal is a choice, not an unavoidable IR stage.
