### 6.4 Why More Context Is Not Always Better ("Lost in the Middle")

**Q27. [Conceptual] Explain Why More Context Is Not Always Better ("Lost in the Middle") in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** Research by Liu et al. ("Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts") demonstrated that LLMs exhibit a strong U-shaped performance curve over long contexts: they reliably attend to information at the very beginning (primacy) and very end (recency) of the prompt, but performance drops sharply when key facts are buried in the middle of a bloated context window.


**Q28. [Interview] Why does Why More Context Is Not Always Better ("Lost in the Middle") matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** Practical Engineering Rule (Scenario 6): Never blindly expand to an entire 4,000-token chapter when your answer-context budget is 2,000 tokens. Set strict expansion limits, expand to the smallest sufficient structural level (e.g., subsection or radius-2 sentence window for "What is the interval?", versus full procedure for "Explain the complete inspection process"), and order the highest-confidence evidence near the boundaries of the prompt.

## 8.5 The 8 Core Doubts Clarified (Doubts D1–D8)

**Q33. [Conceptual] Explain The 8 Core Doubts Clarified (Doubts D1–D8) in simple, interview-ready language.**

**Answer:** No. Recursive chunking follows an ordered hierarchy of natural separators (Section -> Paragraph -> Sentence -> Word) under a configured maximum limit. If a section is 336 tokens and the cap is 400 tokens, it stays one 336-token chunk. If the cap is 100 tokens, it splits at paragraph or sentence boundaries—producing natural-sized pieces (e.g., 85, 92, 78, 81 tokens) rather than mechanical 100, 100, 100, 36 cuts.


**Q34. [Interview] Why does The 8 Core Doubts Clarified (Doubts D1–D8) matter, and what is one important design implication?**

**Answer:** Semantic similarity measures vocabulary/topic closeness, not logical or business dependency. A safety exception ("During cold shutdown, drain all valves...") often uses completely different wording from the routine rule it modifies ("Inspect pressure gauge every 30 days"). Semantic chunking sees a mathematical similarity drop and splits them apart, whereas structure-aware chunking keeps them together under the same heading.
